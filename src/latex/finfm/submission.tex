\title{When Are Foundation-Model Neighbours Economic?\\
Predictive Equivalence, Neighbour Margins and a Spatial APT}
\author{Anonymous Submission}
\affiliations{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Embeddings from tabular foundation models (TFMs) are increasingly read as maps
of firms, with nearest neighbours treated as economic peers. We ask when such
geometry measures an economic relationship rather than merely supporting a
prediction. Defining proximity as the volatility of the difference between two
firms' responses to common shocks, we show that predictive content fixes a
representation only up to the transformations its readout can undo: a
prediction-preserving linear map can reverse nearest neighbours, also after
unit normalisation or coordinate standardisation. A task statistic identifies
an economic target exactly when equal statistics imply equal targets. Given an
exposure readout, a sharp neighbour-margin certificate states when an estimated
k-nearest-neighbour network equals the economic one; these results are
machine-checked in Lean. We then revisit Fern\'andez's characteristic-history
spatial CAPM on CRSP and SEC data and extend it to an out-of-sample spatial APT
whose networks come from four synthetic-prior TFMs, both raw and calibrated to
factor exposures. Calibration raises agreement with estimated exposures, though
not beyond the TFMs' own inputs, and no TFM network improves out-of-sample
covariance scores over a five-factor plus momentum APT, whereas industry links
do.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Financial networks connect firms that should move together: peers for
valuation, neighbours for risk spillovers, links for hedging. Learned
representations offer a tempting shortcut. If a pretrained model encodes each
firm's accounting history, distances between its states could define the
network~\citep{gabaix2025asset,hoberg2016}. The premise is that a model that
predicts well has learned an economically meaningful geometry.

That premise conflates three things. A representation can contain the
information a prediction needs; its distances can mean something economically;
and a finite sample can estimate a readout that turns the first into the
second. We separate them and test the separation on US equities.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\emph{(i) Theory.} We define proximity in common-shock exposure units
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}) and show that predictive equivalence does not
identify this geometry (Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence}), that task success
identifies a target exactly under a factorisation condition
(Proposition~\ref{prop:alignment}), and that a neighbour-margin condition
certifies when an estimated k-nearest-neighbour (k-NN) network equals the
economic one (Theorem~\ref{thm:margin}). The certificate and the counterexample
are machine-checked in Lean~4 together with the alignment and
covariance-stability results. \emph{(ii) Replication.} We reimplement
Fern\'andez's characteristic-history spatial CAPM~\citep{fernandez2011} on
CRSP and SEC data, and show that her high spatial coefficient reflects a
near-uniform kernel absorbing the cross-sectional mean
(Section~\ref{sec:replication}). \emph{(iii) Extension.} We move from CAPM to a
spatial APT~\citep{kou2018} and compare characteristic, raw TFM and
exposure-calibrated TFM networks, separating exposure recovery from
out-of-sample covariance performance (Section~\ref{sec:apt}).

\paragraph{What is not shown.} We do not claim that pretraining helps, that a
model creates information absent from its inputs, or any causal effect. All
empirical results are for one roster and one train/test split, use revised
rather than as-filed disclosures, and are reported whatever their sign.

\section{Economic Proximity}\label{sec:setup}
At a given date, firm $i$ has residual return $u_i=b_i^\top q+e_i$, where
$q\in\R^K$ are centred common shocks with covariance $\Omega\succeq0$, $b_i$ are
exposures and $e_i$ is idiosyncratic. We call
\begin{equation}\label{eq:distance}
  d_E(i,j)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\bigl((b_i-b_j)^\top q\bigr)}
  =\norm{a_i-a_j}_2,
\end{equation}
with risk-scaled exposures $a_i=\Omega^{1/2}b_i$,
the economic distance: the volatility of the difference in two firms' common
responses, in return units. Risk scaling makes it Euclidean, and
$b_i^\top\Omega b_j=\tfrac12(\norm{a_i}^2+\norm{a_j}^2-d_E(i,j)^2)$ ties it to
common covariance. Spatial models use proximity through a row-normalised
weight matrix $W$; in a k-NN network firm $i$ links to its $k$ nearest firms.

An encoder maps a firm's observed history $H$ to a state $z=E(H)\in\R^d$, with
pretrained weights and in-context examples held fixed. A readout class
$\mathcal R$ (for example linear maps, or a TFM's prediction head) turns states
into predictions $h(z)$. Raw TFM geometry uses distances between states $z_i$;
calibrated geometry first fits a readout $\hat a=\hat g(z)$ of exposures and
then uses $\norm{\hat a_i-\hat a_j}$.

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}
\subsection{Predictive equivalence leaves geometry open}
Call encoders $E,E'$ \emph{predictively equivalent} under $\mathcal R$ if
$\{h\circ E:h\in\mathcal R\}=\{h\circ E':h\in\mathcal R\}$. Let
$\mathcal G_{\mathcal R}$ be the group of bijections $g$ of $\R^d$ with
$h\circ g^{-1}\in\mathcal R$ for all $h\in\mathcal R$. For linear or affine
readouts $\mathcal G_{\mathcal R}\supseteq GL(d)$; for unrestricted readouts it
contains every bijection.

\begin{proposition}[Predictive equivalence does not fix
  neighbours]\label{prop:equivalence}
  (a) For every $g\in\mathcal G_{\mathcal R}$, $g\circ E$ is predictively
  equivalent to $E$: $(h\circ g^{-1})(gE(H))=h(E(H))$.
  (b) Suppose $GL(d)\subseteq\mathcal G_{\mathcal R}$ and $d\ge2$. If
  $z_i-z_j$ and $z_i-z_l$ are linearly independent, some $g\in GL(d)$ makes $l$
  strictly nearer to $i$ than $j$, and another makes $j$ strictly nearer.
  (c) If $d_{ij}<d_{il}$, every linear $g$ with condition number
  $\kappa(g)<d_{il}/d_{ij}$ preserves the order; when $z_i-z_j\perp z_i-z_l$
  the bound is attained.
\end{proposition}
\noindent Part (a) is immediate. For (b), map $z_i-z_j$ and $z_i-z_l$ to any two
vectors of chosen lengths in their span and extend by the identity. For (c),
$\norm{g(z_i-z_j)}\le\sigma_{\max}d_{ij}<\sigma_{\min}d_{il}\le
\norm{g(z_i-z_l)}$; stretching $z_i-z_j$ by $\kappa$ attains it.

Table~\ref{tab:normalisation} gives exact examples; the first is a Lean theorem
and all are unit tests. Normalisation helps only against the transformations it
cancels. Unit norm cancels scalars; standardisation cancels diagonal stretches
but not shears; whitening cancels $GL(d)$ but needs $N>d$ and leaves nonlinear
reparametrisations, which a TFM's nonlinear head absorbs. Even an identified
geometry need not be economic: whitened distance equals $d_E$ only if the
economic readout $L$ satisfies $L^\top L\propto\Sigma_z^{-1}$. This generalises
the diagonal rescaling argument of \citet{steck2024} for cosine similarity and
is the geometric face of identifiability up to a group~\citep{roeder2021,
khemakhem2020}; similarity indices deliberately not invariant to $GL(d)$, such
as CKA~\citep{kornblith2019}, inherit the same dependence on parametrisation.

\subsection{When a task identifies the target}
Let $F(H)$ be the statistic an ideal task predictor must recover (the
conditional mean for squared loss, the law for a proper
score~\citep{gneiting2011}) and $s(H)$ the economic target.
\begin{proposition}[Alignment~\citep{achille2018}]\label{prop:alignment}
  Every representation that supports the task ($F=h\circ E$ for some $h$) also
  determines $s$ if and only if $F(H)=F(H')$ implies $s(H)=s(H')$.
\end{proposition}
\noindent ``If'': define $g(F(H))=s(H)$, well defined by assumption; then
$s=g\circ h\circ E$. ``Only if'': take $E=F$. The condition is necessary and
sufficient for \emph{all} task-supporting encoders; a particular encoder may
retain more. Whether it holds is an empirical premise about the task, not a
consequence of predictive accuracy. The TFMs here are conditioned on a
valuation task (next-quarter market equity over assets); two firms with equal
expected valuation ratios can have different factor exposures, so success on
that task cannot by itself certify exposure geometry. With a linear
task-to-exposure link of minimum gain $\kappa$, task error $\varepsilon$ and
misalignment $\nu$ give exposure error at most $(\varepsilon+\nu)/\kappa$
(Appendix~\ref{app:chain}).

\subsection{A neighbour-margin certificate}
Let $S_i$ be firm $i$'s strict k-NN set under distances $d$ and
$\gamma_i=d_{i(k+1)}-d_{i(k)}$ its \emph{margin}: the gap between its $(k{+}1)$th
and $k$th nearest distances.
\begin{theorem}[Neighbour-margin certificate]\label{thm:margin}
  (a) If $\max_j|\hat d_{ij}-d_{ij}|\le\varepsilon<\gamma_i/2$, then
  $\hat S_i=S_i$; at $\gamma_i=2\varepsilon$ this can fail.
  (b) If $\norm{\hat a_j-a_j}\le\delta_j\le\delta$ for all $j$, then
  $|\hat d_{ij}-d_{ij}|\le\delta_i+\delta$, so $\gamma_i>2(\delta_i+\delta)$
  suffices; the same holds with the estimated margin $\hat\gamma_i$ in place of
  $\gamma_i$, which is computable.
  (c) If (a) holds for every firm, the symmetrised, row-normalised k-NN matrix
  satisfies $\hat W=W$, so every spatial estimate computed from it equals its
  value on the economic network.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  For $j\in S_i$ and $l\notin S_i$, $\hat d_{ij}\le d_{ij}+\varepsilon<
  d_{il}-\varepsilon\le\hat d_{il}$; distances $(0,1,3)\to(0,2,2)$ show
  sharpness. (b) is the reverse triangle inequality, applied symmetrically for
  $\hat\gamma_i$. (c) follows because $W$ depends only on the neighbour sets.
\end{proof}
Part (b) with the linear map of Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence}(c) gives the
same message from two sides: neighbours are stable exactly when margins are
large relative to either estimation error or reparametrisation. When only
$m$ rows fail, the directed row-normalised k-NN matrix moves by at most
$\norm{\hat W-W}_F\le\sqrt{2m/k}$, which feeds a covariance-stability bound
for $\Sigma=(I-\lambda W)^{-1}D(I-\lambda W)^{-\top}$ and a Gaussian-score
bound (Appendix~\ref{app:chain}); those bounds assume invertibility and a
resolvent bound rather than deriving them.

\section{Revisiting Fern\'andez's Spatial CAPM}\label{sec:replication}
Fern\'andez~\citep{fernandez2011} links firms whose financial characteristics
co-move: $d_{ij}=\sqrt{2(1-r_{ij})}$, with $r_{ij}$ the Spearman correlation of
characteristic histories, and $W=\operatorname{rownorm}(\exp(-d))$. A
cross-sectional spatial lag regresses each firm's median quarterly excess
return on its MM CAPM beta~\citep{yohai1987} and $Wy$, without an intercept.
Her 1997--2006 data are not available to us, so this is a reimplementation:
we keep her outcome, betas, distance, kernel, estimators and lack of intercept,
and change the sample (CRSP December-2015 top-1{,}000 US issuers, \ffWRoster{}
on the roster and \ffWIssuers{} with enough quarters, followed through exits
for 2016--2025) and the characteristic sources (CRSP market equity with SEC
CompanyFacts books; debt maturity excludes leases, as before 2019). We add an
intercept specification and a stationary quarter bootstrap that refits betas,
medians and spatial models (\ffWGeneratedDraws{} draws), so intervals account
for generated regressors.

weaken identification of spatial coefficients~\citep{lee2004}. This motivates
ordinal k-NN networks, whose stability is governed by margins
(Theorem~\ref{thm:margin}).

\section{A Spatial APT with TFM Geometry}\label{sec:apt}
\paragraph{Design.} The comparison separates three changes: CAPM to APT, adding
a spatial residual structure, and how proximity is measured. All models share
one roster (the \ffWAptIssuers{} issuers of the December-2015 roster observed in
all \ffWAptTrainMonths{} training months), one information set (states from
January 2016, using facts filed before December 2015) and one calendar:
monthly training returns for 2016--2020 and a test period of
\ffWAptTestMonths{} months in 2021--2025 (\ffWAptTestIssuerMonths{}
issuer-months; \ffWAptExiting{} issuers exit). Exposures $b_i$ are OLS loadings
on the five Fama--French factors and momentum~\citep{fama2015,carhart1997},
risk-scaled by the training factor covariance as in~\eqref{eq:distance}. The
covariance models are CAPM, APT ($B\Omega B^\top+D$) and spatial APT
($B\Omega B^\top+PDP^\top$, $P=(I-\lambda W)^{-1}$), with $\lambda$ and $D$
fitted to training residuals by Gaussian maximum likelihood. Every $W$ is a
symmetrised, row-normalised 10-NN graph built from: Fern\'andez's joint
distance; the estimated exposures; raw TFM states (chord distance); calibrated
states (Euclidean distance of a 5-fold cross-fitted ridge readout of $a_i$,
penalty chosen by leave-one-out within training folds); and the same two
constructions on a direct-feature control. Industry (same two-digit SIC) and a
relabelled null are reference networks.

The TFMs are TabPFN~3.5~\citep{hollmann2025}, TabFM~1.1~\citep{kong2026tabfm},
Kumo Tabular~\citep{nvidia2026kumo} and LimiX-2~\citep{zhang2026limix}, with
pinned weights. All four state that they were pretrained only on synthetic
tables, so pretraining cannot have seen these returns. Each encodes two
information sets: ten annual slots of Fern\'andez's four characteristics
(``Histories'') and the latest value of every standard CompanyFacts concept
reported by at least 10\% of issuers (``XBRL''), with 256 labelled support
rows from earlier quarters. No parameter of a raw network is fitted to returns.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Recovery is evaluated on the training window only:
out-of-fold $R^2$ of the readout for $a_i$, and the Spearman Mantel correlation
between each distance and the exposure distance $d_E$ (node bootstrap,
\ffWAptMantelDraws{} draws). Out of sample, each month's observed issuers score
the mean Gaussian log density of their excess returns (zero mean for every
model, so only second moments differ), and we record the realised volatility
of the global minimum-variance portfolio. Paired monthly differences against
APT get stationary-bootstrap intervals (\ffWAptDraws{} draws) and Holm
adjustment over \ffWComparisons{} pre-declared comparisons.

\paragraph{Out-of-sample covariance.} Tables~\ref{tab:apt}
and~\ref{tab:baselines} report the ladder. Relative to APT, CAPM changes the
score by \ffWCapmGain{} \ffWCapmCi{} ($\times10^{-3}$): the five factors and
momentum help on average, but not significantly over 60 months.
Adding a spatial term on TFM geometry does not help: of \ffWFmSpatialCount{}
TFM networks, \ffWFmBetterPoint{} have a positive point estimate,
\ffWFmBetterHolm{} improve significantly after Holm adjustment, and
\ffWFmWorseHolm{} are significantly worse. Calibration improves on raw
geometry with Holm $p<0.05$ in \ffWCalOverRawBetterHolm{} of
\ffWCalOverRawCount{} paired comparisons. Fern\'andez's characteristics
(\ffWCharacteristicsGain{} \ffWCharacteristicsCi) and even the estimated
exposures themselves (\ffWExposureGain{} \ffWExposureCi, despite
$\lambda=\ffWExposureLambda$ in training) add nothing out of sample. Industry
links are the exception: \ffWIndustryGain{} \ffWIndustryCi{} with
$\lambda=\ffWIndustryLambda$, and the lowest minimum-variance volatility
(\ffWIndustryGmv{} against \ffWAptGmv{} for APT).

\paragraph{Reading the evidence.} The pattern is consistent with the theory,
though one split cannot establish it. After five factors and momentum, the
residual co-movement that a network can capture in this sample runs along
industry lines, which neither characteristic histories nor TFM states encode as
nearest neighbours. Exposure-calibrated geometry is closer to the economic
target, but its neighbour sets are far from certified, and a spatial term on
uncertified neighbours plausibly adds estimation noise rather than signal. The
in-sample panels, where TFM networks did show positive spatial coefficients
and higher Mantel correlations with realised return correlations than direct
features (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:insample}), therefore do not translate into
out-of-sample risk gains here.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
Identifiability results show that learned representations are often determined
only up to linear or simple nonlinear maps~\citep{roeder2021,khemakhem2020};
\citet{steck2024} show that diagonal rescaling makes cosine similarity
arbitrary in matrix factorisation, and representation-similarity indices build
in particular invariances~\citep{kornblith2019,huh2024}. Sufficiency
literatures characterise what information a representation keeps, not its
metric~\citep{achille2018}. In finance, economically anchored firm networks
come from product text, supply chains and technology
links~\citep{hoberg2016,cohen2008,lee2019technological}, and learned asset
embeddings from holdings~\citep{gabaix2025asset}; spatial asset pricing uses
characteristic or economic distances~\citep{fernandez2011,kou2018,
fulle2024spatial}. We add a measurement view connecting these strands:
TFM geometry must be anchored, and neighbour networks certified, before it can
stand in for economic proximity.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
The theory is conditional: it assumes an economic target and, for the risk
bounds, invertibility and resolvent bounds that we do not derive. The empirical
results use one December roster, one train/test split and monthly exposures
estimated from 60 observations, which bounds the quality of the exposure
target itself; the training sample requires complete training returns.
CompanyFacts values are revised rather than as filed, industry codes are
current, and model weights postdate the sample, although pretraining used
synthetic data only. The replication departs from Fern\'andez's sample. We
evaluate geometry under one valuation-conditioned prompt per model, so other
prompts or fine-tuning could behave differently. A full multi-formation,
as-filed design is left for future work.

\section{Proofs and Bounds}\label{app:proofs}
\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence}.} (a) $h\circ g^{-1}\in\mathcal R$
by definition of $\mathcal G_{\mathcal R}$, and $(h\circ g^{-1})\circ(g\circ E)
=h\circ E$; the reverse inclusion uses $g^{-1}\in\mathcal G_{\mathcal R}$.
(b) With $u=z_i-z_j$ and $v=z_i-z_l$ linearly independent, extend $\{u,v\}$ to a
basis and define $g$ by $gu=se_1$, $gv=e_2$ and the identity on the remaining
basis vectors, with $(e_1,e_2)$ orthonormal; $s>1$ makes $l$ nearer and $s<1$
makes $j$ nearer. (c) As in the main text; with $u\perp v$, take
$g=I+(\kappa-1)uu^\top/\norm u^2$, so $\norm{gu}=\kappa d_{ij}$ and
$\norm{gv}=d_{il}$, and the order reverses once $\kappa>d_{il}/d_{ij}$.

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:alignment}.} As in the main text; the
nonemptiness of the target space supplies values off the image of $F$.

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:margin}.} (a) For $j\in S_i$, $l\notin S_i\cup\{i\}$,
$\hat d_{ij}-\hat d_{il}\le d_{ij}-d_{il}+2\varepsilon\le2\varepsilon-\gamma_i<0$.
Two strict neighbour sets of equal size coincide, since a member of one outside
the other would be strictly nearer than a member of the other outside the
first. Sharpness: row distances $(0,1,3)$ have margin 2 and move by at most 1 to
$(0,2,2)$, where the set $\{1\}$ is no longer strict. (b) The reverse triangle
inequality gives $|\norm{\hat a_i-\hat a_j}-\norm{a_i-a_j}|\le
\norm{\hat a_i-a_i}+\norm{\hat a_j-a_j}$; apply (a) with the roles of $d$ and
$\hat d$ exchanged for $\hat\gamma_i$. (c) Symmetrisation and row normalisation
are functions of the neighbour sets. For $m$ failing rows of the directed
row-normalised graph, each changed row has at most $k$ entries moving from
$1/k$ to $0$ and $k$ from $0$ to $1/k$, so its squared norm is at most $2/k$.

\subsection{From networks to risk}\label{app:chain}
For $P=(I-\lambda W)^{-1}$ and $\hat P=(I-\hat\lambda\hat W)^{-1}$ with spectral
norms at most $M$, $\norm D_2\le V$, $\norm{\hat W}_2\le B_W$,
$|\hat\lambda-\lambda|\le\varepsilon_\lambda$ and $\norm{\hat D-D}_2\le
\varepsilon_D$,
\begin{align*}
  &\norm{\hat P\hat D\hat P^\top-PDP^\top}_2\\
  &\quad\le2VM^3\bigl(|\lambda|\norm{\hat W-W}_2+\varepsilon_\lambda B_W\bigr)
  +M^2\varepsilon_D.
\end{align*}
If a positive-definite forecast $S\succeq mI$ is within $e<m$ of a
positive-definite target $M_0$ in spectral norm, the Gaussian score gap
satisfies $0\le L(S;M_0)-L(M_0;M_0)\le N(e/m)^2/(2(1-e/m))$ with
$L(C;M)=\log\det C+\operatorname{tr}(C^{-1}M)$. Both are Lean theorems
(Appendix~\ref{app:lean}). For the exponential kernel, distance errors bounded
by $b$ after scaling give $\norm{\hat W-W}_\infty\le2(e^b-1)/(e^b+1)$ and hence
$\norm{\hat W-W}_2\le\sqrt N\cdot2(e^b-1)/(e^b+1)$; the $\sqrt N$ comes from a
norm conversion and is not claimed to be sharp. The task-to-exposure bound
$\norm{\hat L\hat\tau_i-a_i}\le(\varepsilon_\tau+\nu)/\kappa+\varepsilon_LB_\tau$
holds for task means $\tau_i=Ca_i+v_i$ with $\norm{Ca}\ge\kappa\norm a$, task
error $\varepsilon_\tau$, misalignment $\norm{v_i}\le\nu$ and a fitted decoder
within $\varepsilon_L$ of a left inverse, with $\norm{\hat\tau_i}\le B_\tau$.

\subsection{Normalisation examples}\label{app:examples}
Raw: $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $C=(0,2)$; $d_{AB}=1<d_{AC}=2$, and after
$\diag(3,1)$, $d_{AB}=3>2$. Unit norm: $A=(1,1)$, $B=(1,0)$, $C=(1,4)$ have
$\cos^2(A,B)=\tfrac12<\cos^2(A,C)=\tfrac{25}{34}$; after $\diag(4,1)$ they are
$\tfrac{16}{17}>\tfrac{25}{34}$. Standardisation: for $A=(0,0)$, $B=(-2,-2)$,
$C=(-2,-1)$, $D=(-2,2)$, squared standardised distances from $A$ make $C$
nearest ($\tfrac{608}{105}$, against $\tfrac{752}{105}$ for $B$); any diagonal
stretch leaves this unchanged, but after the shear $(x,y)\mapsto(x+y,y)$ and
re-standardisation $D$ is nearest ($\tfrac{64}{35}$).
