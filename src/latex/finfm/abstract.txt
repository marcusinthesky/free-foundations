Embeddings from tabular foundation models (TFMs) are increasingly read as maps of firms, with nearest neighbours treated as economic peers. We ask when such geometry measures an economic relationship rather than merely supporting a prediction. Defining proximity as the volatility of the difference between two firms' responses to common shocks, we show that predictive content fixes a representation only up to the transformations its readout can undo: a prediction-preserving linear map can reverse nearest neighbours, also after unit normalisation or coordinate standardisation. A task statistic identifies an economic target exactly when equal statistics imply equal targets. Given an exposure readout, a sharp neighbour-margin certificate states when an estimated k-nearest-neighbour network equals the economic one; these results are machine-checked in Lean. We then revisit Fernández's characteristic-history spatial CAPM on CRSP and SEC data and extend it to an out-of-sample spatial APT whose networks come from four synthetic-prior TFMs, both raw and calibrated to factor exposures. Calibration raises agreement with estimated exposures, though not beyond the TFMs' own inputs, and no TFM network improves out-of-sample covariance scores over a five-factor plus momentum APT, whereas industry links do.
